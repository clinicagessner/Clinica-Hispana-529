# High Blood Pressure Without Symptoms: Silent Killer

"My blood pressure is fine, nothing ever hurts." It's one of the lines heard most often in a waiting room, and it usually comes from someone who hasn't had a reading in years. That is exactly the trap. High blood pressure almost never announces itself. It causes no fever, no burning, no loss of appetite. Meanwhile, every heartbeat pushes blood a little too hard against the artery walls, and that extra strain quietly adds up over years.

That's where the nickname "silent killer" comes from. Not because hypertension is mysterious, but because when nobody has been looking for it, the first sign can be a heart attack, a stroke or kidneys that no longer filter well. According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/high-blood-pressure/data-research/facts-stats/index.html), nearly half of U.S. adults have high blood pressure, and a significant share of them are unaware of it.

## What happens inside while you feel nothing

Think of a garden hose left on full blast all day: over time the rubber stiffens and cracks. Arteries react in a similar way: constant high pressure makes them rigid and makes it easier for fatty deposits to build up along their walls. From there, the damage spreads to several organs:

- **Heart:** it pumps against more resistance, the muscle thickens, and over the years it can weaken or suffer a heart attack.
- **Brain:** a blocked or burst artery causes a stroke, and high blood pressure is one of its main drivers.
- **Kidneys:** their tiny vessels get injured and filter less, often without any visible change in the urine.
- **Eyes:** retinal vessels suffer too, and vision can be affected.

None of this hurts at first.

## What about the neck ache or the nosebleed?

Plenty of people believe they can tell when their pressure "goes up" because the back of the neck aches, they feel dizzy or their face flushes. Those complaints may happen alongside a high reading, but they are not a reliable gauge: some people with very high numbers feel perfectly well, and others have a headache with a normal reading. Going by how you feel leads to two common mistakes: never getting checked because "there are no symptoms," or taking the pill only on headache days. If you already have a treatment plan, talk with the medical team before changing anything about how you take it.

## The numbers worth knowing

Blood pressure is written as two numbers. The top one (systolic) is the force while the heart beats; the bottom one (diastolic) is the pressure while it rests between beats. The categories used by the CDC and the American Heart Association are:

- **Normal:** under 120 on top and under 80 on the bottom.
- **Elevated:** 120 to 129 on top, with the bottom under 80.
- **Stage 1 hypertension:** 130 to 139 on top, or 80 to 89 on the bottom.
- **Stage 2 hypertension:** 140 or more on top, or 90 or more on the bottom.

Only one of the two numbers needs to fall into a category for it to count. A reading of 128/86, for example, is already stage 1 because of the bottom number. One high reading is not a diagnosis; several on different days start to show a pattern that the clinic's medical team reviews with you.

## How often should you check it?

There's no single schedule: it depends on your age, your past readings and your history. As general guidance:

1. **Young adult with normal readings and no other conditions:** at least every one to two years, for instance during a routine checkup or a work physical.
2. **From age 40, or with risk factors** (extra weight, diabetes, high cholesterol, parents with hypertension, loud snoring, lots of salt or alcohol): at least once a year.
3. **If a reading already came back "elevated" or stage 1:** the medical team will tell you when to repeat it and will often ask for home readings to confirm.
4. **If you're already diagnosed and treated:** when a medication is started or changed, the review happens in 4 to 6 weeks; once your pressure is controlled, visits repeat every 3 to 6 months. Between visits, regular home readings help show the trend.

## Measuring at home without fooling yourself

An automatic upper-arm monitor is more dependable than a wrist one. What throws readings off is usually not the device but how it's used:

- skip coffee, cigarettes and exercise for half an hour beforehand, and empty your bladder first;
- sit with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed;
- rest quietly for five minutes, phone put away;
- wrap the cuff on bare skin, not over a sleeve, with your arm resting on a table at heart level;
- stay silent during the measurement, take two readings one minute apart and write both down with the date and time.

Cuff size matters as well: one that squeezes too tightly or sits loose will skew the numbers. Bring your monitor to the visit to compare it with the clinic's.

Two situations make out-of-office readings worthwhile. Some people get nervous at the clinic and read higher than they do at home; others read normal at the clinic and high in daily life. Only a log covering several days can tell the two apart.

## Small changes that actually move the numbers

In many Latin kitchens, salt hides where you'd least expect it: the bouillon powder in the soup, deli meats, instant noodles, bottled sauces and bakery bread. Seasoning with garlic, onion, cilantro, lime and chiles instead of the bouillon cube already cuts a lot. A brisk half-hour walk most days, losing some weight if there's extra, easing up on alcohol and sleeping better all show up on the monitor too. These changes help, but they don't replace any treatment you've been prescribed.

## When not to wait: call 911

If a reading shows 180/120 or higher, sit down, wait a few minutes and measure again. If it stays that high even though you feel fine, contact the medical team promptly. But if the high number comes with chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, one side of the face drooping, a weak arm, slurred speech or suddenly blurred vision, **call 911**: don't drive yourself and don't come to the clinic.
