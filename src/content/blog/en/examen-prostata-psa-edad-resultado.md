# Prostate Exam and PSA: When to Test and What It Means

A 52-year-old man comes in because his older brother was just told something was found in his prostate. He feels fine, urinates without trouble and has never had the test. He brings three questions we hear almost every week: what exactly is PSA, am I old enough to need it, and if it comes back high, does that mean I have cancer?

All three have answers, and none of them is a simple yes or no. This guide explains what the test measures, how the starting age is decided, which everyday things can move the number and what comes next when the value is not what you expected.

## First, the gland: what the prostate does

The prostate is a walnut-sized gland that sits below the bladder and wraps around the urethra, the tube urine flows through. It makes part of the fluid in semen. With age it almost always grows a little, which is why many men past 50 notice they get up at night to urinate or that the stream is weaker. That growth, called benign prostatic hyperplasia, is very common and is not cancer.

## What PSA measures, and what it does not

PSA stands for prostate-specific antigen, a protein made by the prostate itself. A small amount reaches the bloodstream, and that is what the test measures: a sample from the arm, just like any other lab test. No fasting is needed.

The key point is that PSA is not a cancer test. It is a signal that the prostate is releasing more of this protein than usual, and that can happen for several reasons:

- The prostate has grown with age
- The gland is inflamed or infected (prostatitis)
- There was a recent urinary tract infection
- There is a tumor, which is only one of the possibilities

That is why the number is never read on its own. It is weighed together with your age, your symptoms, your family history and any earlier results you have.

## What about the rectal exam?

People say "prostate exam" for two different things. One is the PSA blood test. The other is a physical check of the gland. Current guidelines treat PSA as the starting point for screening, and the physical check is no longer recommended as routine for men without symptoms. If what you describe or your result suggests the prostate should be looked at another way, the visit is where the team works out which study makes sense and explains it before anything is done. The [kidney and bladder ultrasound](/en/services/ultrasonido) done at the clinic, for example, includes a look at the prostate and at how much urine stays in the bladder.

## What age to talk about PSA

There is no magic age that fits everyone. Public health recommendations in the United States agree that this is a decision made in conversation, weighing benefits against downsides, and that personal risk shifts the timeline.

| Your situation | When to bring it up |
|---|---|
| No family history, no symptoms | Between 55 and 69, as a shared decision |
| Father or brother with prostate cancer | From around 45 |
| African American man | From around 45 |
| Several relatives diagnosed young | Can be considered from 40 |
| Over 70 with no symptoms | Generally not done routinely; discussed case by case |
| Urinary trouble at any age | Checked at the visit, without waiting for screening age |

Why not test every man every year from a young age? Because a high PSA leads to more studies, and some prostate tumors grow so slowly they would never have caused harm. The conversation is there so you decide with information, not out of fear.

## Before the draw: what can move the number

PSA reacts to ordinary things. For the result to reflect what is really going on, keep the following in mind:

1. Avoid ejaculation for the two days before the sample, because it raises the value for a while.
2. If you rode a bicycle or motorcycle for a long stretch, or had a very hard workout in the days before, mention it.
3. If you had a urinary or prostate infection, the test waits until treatment is finished, since inflammation pushes it up.
4. Bring a list of what you take. Some treatments for an enlarged prostate or for hair loss can cut PSA roughly in half, and that changes how the number is read.
5. If you have results from earlier years, bring them. The trend matters as much as today's value.

## How to read the number

The result is reported in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL). For years 4 was used as the dividing line, but today we know there is no cutoff that cleanly separates normal from abnormal. A few things the medical team at Clínica 529 looks at:

- Age: a value that raises concern at 45 can be expected at 70, because the gland grows.
- Rate of change: a PSA that keeps climbing from one year to the next says more than any single figure.
- Context: a high value with fever and painful urination points first to inflammation or infection.
- The gray zone: between 4 and 10 ng/mL most men do not have cancer, yet that range still deserves follow-up.

A low result is not a lifetime pass either. It is a snapshot of right now, and how often to repeat it depends on your age and risk.

## If it comes back high: what happens next

First, don't panic. Most often the next step is repeating the test in a few weeks, once whatever might have raised it is ruled out. A [urine test](/en/services/infecciones-urinarias) is often done too, to look for infection, because treating it and measuring again clears up a lot.

If the value stays high or keeps rising, additional studies are discussed, and at that point there is a specialist referral when results call for it. All along, you get a plain explanation in Spanish or English of what each number means and why each step is suggested. ## Symptoms that should not wait for a checkup

Screening is for men who feel well. If you already have symptoms, don't wait to reach the age in the table. It is worth coming in if you find yourself urinating many times at night, straining to start, a stream that stops and starts, a feeling that the bladder never empties, blood in the urine or semen, or lasting pain in the lower back or pelvis.

Some situations are not for a regular visit. If you suddenly cannot pass any urine and your lower belly turns hard and painful, or you have a high fever with shaking chills and burning when you urinate, go to the emergency room or call 911.
