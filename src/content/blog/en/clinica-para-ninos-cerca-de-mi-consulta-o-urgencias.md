# Kids' Clinic Near Me: Clinic Visit or the ER?

It's seven on a Saturday morning. Your six-year-old woke up with a red throat and a warm forehead, won't touch breakfast, and her little brother has started coughing too. Many parents grab their phone and type "kids' clinic near me", and right behind that search comes the real question: can a clinic visit handle this, or is it time to head for the emergency room?

The answer rarely comes down to the number on the thermometer. It depends far more on how your child looks and acts. This guide walks through which children's problems the medical team at a family clinic like ours on FM 529 can look at, and which warning signs mean calling 911 or going straight to the ER.

## Three doors for three different situations

Before buckling the kids into the car, try to place the problem in one of these groups:

| Your child's situation | Where to go |
|---|---|
| Fever, but awake, playing on and off and drinking fluids | Family clinic visit |
| Sore throat, cough, runny nose, hives or diarrhea with no red flags | Family clinic visit |
| A cut that may need stitches, with bleeding under control | Family clinic visit |
| Baby under 3 months with 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher | ER, without waiting |
| Struggling to breathe, blue lips, a seizure or hard to wake | Call 911 |

The reasoning is simple. A family clinic is built for illnesses and injuries that worry you but don't threaten your child's life right now. The ER and 911 are for anything that can't wait even a little while.

## The most useful question: how does your child look?

A child at 102 °F who, once the fever comes down, sits up to watch cartoons and asks for juice is usually less sick than a child at 100 °F who won't lift their head off the pillow. Before deciding, look at four things:

1. **Whether they play or respond** at least a little when the fever drops.
2. **Whether they drink fluids** and keep peeing regularly.
3. **How they breathe:** the ribs shouldn't sink in with each breath, and the nostrils shouldn't flare.
4. **The color of their skin and lips:** pink, not gray or blue.

If all four look fine, a clinic visit is most likely the right place. If any of them doesn't, keep reading.

## Signs that call for 911 or the ER

General child-health guidance, including the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/signs-symptoms/index.html), agrees on these warning signs. If you see any of them, don't come to the clinic: call 911 or go to the emergency room.

- A baby younger than 3 months with a temperature of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher, even if the baby seems calm.
- Fast or labored breathing, loud wheezing, or blue lips or fingernails.
- A seizure, fainting, or a child who is hard to wake or doesn't recognize their parents.
- Signs of dehydration: a dry diaper for many hours, crying without tears, a very dry mouth or a sunken soft spot in babies.
- Fever with a stiff neck, or red or purple spots on the skin that don't fade when you press a glass against them.
- Green or bloody vomit, or belly pain so strong the child can't walk upright.
- A blow to the head followed by vomiting, unusual sleepiness or confusion.
- Swelling of the lips, tongue or throat after eating something or after a sting.
- A wound that keeps bleeding despite firm pressure, or with bone showing.

If you think your child swallowed a medicine, a cleaning product or a button battery, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222, or 911 if the child has symptoms.

## Fever, without the panic

Fever itself is one of the body's defenses, and it does not "burn" a child's brain. The two things worth tracking are the number of days it sticks around and the symptoms that travel with it. As a general guide, have the medical team take a look if the fever lasts more than three days, comes and goes over a week, or shows up together with ear pain, throat pain or pain when peeing.

To bring it down, don't give adult medicines or combine products on your own: the right amount depends on the child's weight, and the medical team works that out during the visit. Skip rubbing alcohol on the skin and cold-water baths as well.

To go back to school or daycare, the rule is a full day without fever and without anything given to bring it down.
