# Which Ultrasound Do I Need? A Symptom-Based Guide

A question the front desk hears all the time: "I want an ultrasound. Which one should I get?" It makes sense to ask, because "ultrasound" covers several quite different exams. The machine looks similar, but what gets examined when your stomach hurts after dinner is not what gets examined after a positive pregnancy test or when you find a lump in your neck. Each area has its own study, and some of them ask you to arrive fasting or with a full bladder.

This guide starts from how you feel rather than from the name of the test, so you walk into the visit already knowing which study is typically ordered for your situation. The clinic's medical team makes the final call after listening to you and examining you, since two people with the same pain can end up needing different studies.

## Rule out an emergency first

Before worrying about which ultrasound fits, make sure you don't need the ER. Any of these signs means 911 or the nearest emergency room, not a clinic visit:

- severe belly pain that keeps you from moving or is getting worse quickly;
- a high fever together with abdominal pain;
- vomiting that won't stop, to the point you can't keep water down;
- pregnancy with heavy bleeding, intense pain or fainting.

In those cases you need immediate care, and a scheduled clinic study is the wrong route.

## "My upper belly hurts, mostly after I eat"

This is one of the most frequent reasons people come in. Picture Mario, who gets a sharp pain under his right ribs every time he eats something greasy, sometimes spreading to his back. For a story like his, the usual choice is an **abdominal ultrasound**.

According to the clinic's service page, the abdominal study looks at the liver, the gallbladder and gallstones, the pancreas, the spleen, the kidneys and the aorta. That's why it tends to be ordered when the pain sits in the upper abdomen or on the right side after meals, the classic gallbladder complaint.

You'll be asked to fast for 6 to 8 hours beforehand, and water is fine. Fasting keeps the gallbladder full and cuts down on gas that blurs the picture. If your visit is in the afternoon, eat an early breakfast and then nothing more until the study is done.

Not every stomachache needs imaging. Heartburn, gastritis or constipation are often sorted out during the visit itself, and the medical team will tell you whether an ultrasound would actually add anything.

## "I have low pelvic pain or bleeding that isn't normal for me"

When the discomfort sits below the belly button and comes with very heavy periods, bleeding between periods or pain that returns every month, the study usually ordered is a **pelvic ultrasound**. The clinic uses it to look at the uterus and ovaries, check for cysts or fibroids, and investigate causes of pain or bleeding.

This one needs a full bladder: drink 3 to 4 glasses of water an hour ahead and hold it. A full bladder gives a clearer view of the pelvic organs.

The pelvic study is often part of a broader [women's health and gynecology visit](/en/services/ginecologia), where periods, birth control or a Pap test can be covered too.

## "My pregnancy test came back positive"

If the home test showed two lines, the first step is confirming it. The clinic offers a [urine or blood pregnancy test](/en/services/prueba-embarazo), and a **pregnancy ultrasound** can follow. The service page describes it as a way to confirm the pregnancy, see the heartbeat, find out how many babies there are, estimate the due date and track progress.

Early in pregnancy a full bladder is needed; after week 12 it isn't. Because ultrasound relies on sound waves and not radiation, it's considered safe during pregnancy.

An ultrasound doesn't take the place of prenatal care. It's one part of it, alongside prenatal vitamins, lab work and regular checkups. If you're pregnant and notice heavy bleeding, intense one-sided pain or dizziness, skip the appointment and head to the ER.

## "I found a lump in my neck" or "my thyroid labs came back off"

Sometimes a partner notices it during a hug, or you spot it while shaving: a swelling at the base of the neck that rises when you swallow. Other times there's no lump at all, just a blood test showing a TSH out of range. Either way, the study usually ordered is a **thyroid ultrasound**.

It measures the size of the gland and shows whether there are nodules. No prep is required, so eat and drink as you normally would. The clinic's [thyroid care page](/en/services/tiroides) explains that ultrasound is used when nodules need a closer look, alongside TSH, free T4 and T3 testing.

A nodule is not automatically bad news. They're common, and many are simply monitored. The medical team explains what the images showed and, when a case calls for another study or an outside evaluation, guides the referral.

## Two more situations with their own study

Most questions revolve around the belly, the pelvis, pregnancy or the neck, but the clinic performs two other types worth knowing about:

- **Kidneys and bladder.** Blood in your urine, or a hard cramp in your side that moves down toward the groin, may lead to a kidney and bladder ultrasound. It looks for stones and urine retention and, in men, also evaluates the prostate. Like the pelvic study, it needs a full bladder. If there's burning too, the [urinary infection visit](/en/services/infecciones-urinarias) includes a urine test.
- **Lumps under the skin.** A small bump on the back, arm or nape of the neck may be a cyst or a lipoma. A soft tissue ultrasound helps tell what it is before deciding whether to remove it with minor surgery. No preparation is needed.

## When your symptom isn't on this list

Some complaints don't match any of these studies. The clinic performs only the types described on its ultrasound page: abdominal, pelvic, pregnancy, thyroid, soft tissue, and kidney and bladder. If your case calls for different imaging, such as an X-ray, a CT scan or an ultrasound of another body part, the medical team will say so plainly and point you to where you can get it.

That's why a regular visit is the best starting point. You describe what you feel, since when and what makes it worse, and from there the team decides whether you need a study and which one. Sometimes the answer is that no ultrasound is needed at all.

## What the exam feels like, whichever type you get

The basics are the same for every type. You lie down, warm gel goes on the skin, and a handheld transducer glides over the area. You may feel some pressure, but it doesn't hurt. The study takes 15 to 30 minutes, and the medical team reviews the images and walks you through the findings in Spanish.

A few notes on your phone help the team pick the right study:

- where the discomfort is and how long you've had it;
- whether it changes with meals, your period or urination;
- the date of your last period, if it applies;
- any earlier labs or scans related to the problem, even from another country;
- the medications you take.
