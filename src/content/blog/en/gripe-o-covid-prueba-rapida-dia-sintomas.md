# Flu or COVID? Which Rapid Test to Get and When

The chills start on a Tuesday night, by Wednesday morning there's a fever and a dry cough, and the question at home is always the same: is this the flu or COVID? The two look so much alike that even the clinic's medical team can't tell them apart just by looking at a patient. What does make the difference is **when** you test. A rapid test taken at the wrong moment can come back negative even when the virus is there.

This guide is written for families in northwest Houston, from Copperfield, Cypress and Bear Creek, who want to know which test to ask for depending on how many days they've been sick.

## Why the day of symptoms changes the result

Rapid tests look for pieces of the virus in your nose. If there isn't much virus, the test misses it. And the amount of virus in your nose isn't the same every day:

- **Flu:** the virus usually peaks early. That's why the rapid flu test is most accurate during the first 3 to 4 days of symptoms. Wait a week to get tested and it may read negative even if the flu was the cause.
- **COVID-19:** in some people the virus takes a little longer to build up. Testing is recommended starting on the first day of symptoms, but a very early negative isn't always the final word. If you're still sick, test again two days later.

Put simply: with the flu, don't wait; with COVID, don't trust a single negative while you still feel sick.

## Your testing calendar, day by day

- **Exposed to someone sick, no symptoms:** COVID test about 5 days after the exposure. Testing the very next day is usually too soon.
- **Day 1 of symptoms:** flu and COVID together. If COVID is negative and you still feel bad, retest two days later.
- **Days 2 to 4:** flu and COVID together. This is the best window for the flu test.
- **Day 5 and beyond:** the COVID test is still useful; the flu test is less reliable. The exam and a lung check matter more than the test.
- **Bad sore throat, no cough:** strep test. Cough, hoarseness and a runny nose point more toward a virus.

Count day 1 as the day you noticed your first symptom, even if it was something mild like fatigue or body aches.

## Step by step: what to do in your situation

### If your symptoms started a day or two ago

This is the best time to come in. A single nasal swab can run the influenza A and B test and the COVID-19 test, with results in minutes. You also catch the treatment window: flu antivirals work best when started within the first two days of symptoms, and for COVID-19 there's antiviral treatment for people with risk factors if it's started within the first 5 days.

### If you've been sick for three or four days

Both tests are still worth doing. The flu test is still useful at this stage, and knowing which virus you have helps you decide how long to stay home and who you need to tell.

### If it's been five days or more

By now the question shifts. More than the name of the virus, what matters is whether you're getting better or worse. A cough that's getting worse, a fever that comes back after breaking, or shortness of breath can point to a complication like bronchitis or pneumonia. During the visit we listen to your lungs and check your oxygen level, which at this point tell us more than a rapid test.

### If you were only exposed

If someone in your home tested positive but you feel fine, don't rush to test on the day of the exposure. For COVID, testing is recommended about 5 days after close contact, or sooner if symptoms show up.

## What about a home test?

Home COVID tests are handy and useful, but they have the same timing limits: a negative on day one doesn't rule out infection. If you use one at home and it's negative while you have symptoms, repeat it two days later. Also keep in mind that a home test only answers one question, the COVID one. At the clinic, flu and COVID are run from the same sample, and if a sore throat is your main symptom, we can add a [rapid strep test](/services/prueba-strep), since strep is a bacterial infection that does get treated with antibiotics.

## What your result means

**Positive for flu.** The flu is contagious from one day before symptoms start until 5 to 7 days after. If you're within the first two days of symptoms, the clinic's medical team will decide whether an antiviral makes sense for you, especially if you have diabetes or asthma, are over 65 or are pregnant.

**Positive for COVID-19.** Stay home until you've gone a full day without a fever (without taking fever medicine) and your symptoms are improving. After that, wear a mask for about 5 more days around other people.

**Negative for both.** That doesn't always mean it's neither one. A test done too early or too late can miss the virus. It could also be a common cold, RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) or another infection. Colds and viral bronchitis are treated by easing symptoms; antibiotics are only prescribed when there's a bacterial infection.

**Positive for both.** It's uncommon, but it happens. That's one more reason to run both tests at the same time instead of stopping at the first one that turns positive.

## What to jot down before you come in

Having this information ready makes the visit faster and more accurate:

1. The day and approximate time your symptoms started.
2. Your highest temperature and what you took for the fever.
3. Whether anyone at home or at work has been sick or tested positive.
4. If you already took a home test, when you took it and what it showed.
5. Your medication list and whether you have diabetes, high blood pressure, asthma or another condition. People with [chronic conditions](/services/condiciones-cronicas) face a higher risk of complications and may need antiviral treatment.

## When not to wait for a test: warning signs

Call 911 or go straight to the emergency room if you or a family member has:

- Trouble breathing or very fast breathing
- Pain or pressure in the chest
- Bluish lips or face
- Confusion, unusual sleepiness or trouble waking up
- A fever that won't come down with medicine
- Any fever in a baby younger than 3 months

No rapid test should delay care in these situations.
